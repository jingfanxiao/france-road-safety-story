# Demo video script (about 3 minutes)

**Setup before recording:** open the deployed app, default selection (2019–2024, all of
France), sidebar visible, browser zoom ~90% so the charts fit. Close the *Context* expander.
Figures in the narration are those shown for the default selection; if the data is
updated, read them from the screen.

---

### 0:00 – 0:20 · Hook (Story & Overview)

**Screen:** title and hook paragraph.

> "Between 2019 and 2024, French police recorded more than 327,000 injury road accidents,
> and almost 20,000 people died — about 63 every week. But an accident is not equally
> dangerous everywhere. This app asks where, and for whom, an accident is most likely to be
> fatal. It is built for local road-safety decision makers."

### 0:20 – 0:50 · Context and trend

**Screen:** open the *Context* expander briefly, close it, then scroll to the KPI row and the
trend chart. Hover over 2020 on the chart.

> "The key metric is lethality: deaths per 100 injury accidents. It comes straight from the
> official BAAC files on data.gouv.fr, without needing traffic data.
> The headline is computed from the data: accidents fell by 7.5%, but deaths only by 1.9%,
> so lethality went up from 5.9 to 6.3. The chart indexes both series to 2019; 2020, the
> lockdown year, stands out with a sharp drop in both."

### 0:50 – 1:40 · Where

**Screen:** go to *Where*. Show the map on *Accidents per year*, then switch the radio to
*Lethality*. Hover over Paris, then over a rural département such as Creuse or Jura. Scroll
to the top 10 bar chart and point at the grey bar.

> "Do the départements with the most accidents have the deadliest ones? No — it is the
> opposite. On accident volume, the big cities are darkest. Switch to lethality and the map
> flips: rural départements light up. In the quarter of départements with the fewest
> accidents, an accident is 3.3 times as lethal as in the busiest quarter.
> The top 10 ranks départements by lethality; grey bars rest on fewer than 100 accidents, so
> their rate is not reliable."

### 1:40 – 2:00 · What-if

**Screen:** open the *What if…* expander, move the slider from 10 to 5 and to 20.

> "A simple what-if: if the N deadliest départements had the national lethality, about 154
> deaths a year would be avoided for N = 10. It is arithmetic to show the scale, not a
> prediction."

### 2:00 – 2:30 · Who and when

**Screen:** go to *Who*. Hover over the *Pedestrian* and *Car* bars. Scroll to the heatmap,
then in the sidebar change *Indicator* to *Lethality*.

> "Pedestrians are the most exposed: about 5 in 100 involved in an injury accident died, more
> than twice the rate of car occupants. Together, users outside a car account for almost half
> of the deaths. The heatmap shows accidents peak at 5 pm on weekdays — but switching the
> indicator to lethality moves the dark cells to the night: the deadliest hour is 5 am."

### 2:30 – 2:50 · Filters

**Screen:** in the sidebar, choose Region = *Bretagne* and years 2022–2024; go back to
*Story & Overview* to show the KPIs and headline change. Click *Reset filters*.

> "Every KPI, headline and chart follows the sidebar, so a département or a region can
> retell the story for its own territory."

### 2:50 – 3:10 · Data quality and conclusions

**Screen:** go to *Data Quality & Conclusions*; scroll through the checks table, the
missingness chart, the three conclusions and the limitations.

> "The raw files pass most checks; the warnings are documented and handled. Three takeaways:
> look at deaths and lethality, not just counts; rank territories by lethality as well as
> volume; and protect road users outside a car. These are associations, not causes — without
> traffic data, lethality is severity, not risk per inhabitant. Thank you."

---

**Tips:** speak slowly; let each hover tooltip stay visible for a second; keep the mouse
still while talking about a chart. Target length 3:00, acceptable 2:30–3:30.
